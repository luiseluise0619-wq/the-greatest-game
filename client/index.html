        HOW A ROUND GOES

        • Roles are secret. Sheriff, Deputies, Outlaws, one Renegade. Nobody is told who anybody is.
        • You get one thread to pull. A Deputy learns two names, one of which is the Sheriff. An Outlaw recognises a single accomplice.
        • Kills only name the killer if somebody watched it happen. Everything else is a rumour and a location.
        • The dead reveal their role. Every body is evidence — for whoever is still standing.
        • The Sheriff may pin on the star for real armour and a permanent target. Or stay a stranger.
        • The Sheriff wears the star from the bell. He is the one man everybody can find, and the only role in this mode that is not a secret.
        • You take it in turns, standing at a table. Nobody walks: one man at a time gets six seconds with the only live gun in town, and distance is how many seats away you are.
        • Ammunition is cards. You cannot fire without a Bang! in your hand, your gun reaches exactly as far as the card in front of you says, and the chamber the whole town is drawing from has blanks in it.

        HOW THIS IS PLAYED

        Read it now. Nobody stops for questions once the bell has rung.

        WHAT YOU ARE TRYING TO DO

        Roles are secret and they decide everything. The Sheriff wants the town clean. His Deputies want him alive, and he does not know who they are. The Outlaws want the Sheriff dead. The Renegade wants to be the last man standing, which means he needs the Outlaws gone and the Sheriff dead — last.

        Everybody gets one thread to pull. A Deputy learns two names, one of which is the Sheriff. An Outlaw recognises a single accomplice. The Sheriff knows nothing at all. Everything else you work out by watching who shoots whom.

        A killing only names the killer if somebody watched it happen. Otherwise the town gets a shot, a direction and a rumour. The dead turn their role face up, which makes every body a piece of evidence for whoever is still standing.

        THE TABLE

        This is played standing at a table in the middle of Main Street. You are dealt a mark on the ground and that is where you stay — nobody walks, all round. Distance is seats: the man beside you is one away, the man opposite is three or four, and the gun you are holding reaches however many the card in front of you says. Where you were put is as much the hand you were dealt as the cards are.

        A LAP

        One man at a time gets his go, six seconds each, in an order the whole table was shown at the start. Between one lap and the next there is a beat while the chamber is loaded in the open and everybody counts what went into it. You can turn your head whenever you like. On somebody else's go that is all you can do — and it is enough, because the only warning anybody gets is watching him level it.

        WHAT IT TAKES TO FIRE

        Three things, all at once. It has to be your go. You have to be holding a Bang! — one a turn, and it is spent whether or not you hit anything. And the man has to be inside what your gun reaches, which is the card lying in front of you, not the sights.

        Then you have to hold the barrel on him for most of a second before it will go off. That is the draw, and it is the only warning he gets. Anybody watching you can see who you have picked.

        THE CHAMBER

        There is one chamber for the whole town. It is loaded at the start of every lap and announced out loud — so many live, so many blank, never the order — and every shot anybody fires draws the next round. Six people spend the lap counting the same six rounds. A blank is smoke and noise, and it still costs the card.

        WHEN IT STOPS ON YOU

        The screen tells you a barrel has settled on you and you have the length of his draw. Press Space and you are not standing where he thought — but it spends a Missed! out of your hand, and only works if you had one. Nobody spends it for you. It is the only move anybody makes on somebody else's go.

        THE BARREL TURNED ROUND

        On your own go you may put it against your own head with Q. A blank buys you another go on the spot. A live round costs you a hit and carries on out of your back into whoever is standing in line behind you — which at a round table means the man on your other side. Turn to face your left-hand neighbour and it is your right-hand one who catches it. It is a round like any other once it is out of you: a barrel behind you still stops it, and so does a man who happened to be moving.

        THE MAN YOU WERE DEALT

        As well as a role you are dealt one of sixteen gunhands, and it is on your role card. One of them bleeds a card into his own hand every time he is hit. One takes two Missed! to get out of the way of. One is never holding nothing. One goes through the pockets of everybody who goes down. Nobody is told which one anybody else got — but most of them are visible the first time they matter.

        THE EIGHTY

        Your hand is the size of your health, and you draw two at the start of every go — so the closer you are to dying, the less you can do about it. Play one with the number key printed on it — one to nine, then 0 for a tenth. Some need somebody in your crosshair first. Bang! and Missed! are not played with a key: one is fired and one is spent for you when you move.

        READING THE TABLE

        Nobody's hand is public, but how many cards they are holding is, and so is everything face up in front of them — the gun they laid down, the barrel they are stood behind, the horse that keeps them a step further off. All of it is on the running order across the top, beside each name, and it changes as the lap goes round. A man down to one card has almost nothing to answer with.

        Above your own hand is what you may keep: anything over that goes on the pile at the end of your go, so a card held back is a card thrown away. The number beside it is what is left in the pile everybody draws from.

        THE DECK

        Two of six cards are dealt to you every round and nobody is ever told what anybody else holds. None of them shoot — they edit what the town is allowed to know. Play one with Z or X.

        THE STAR

        The Sheriff may pin it on at any time with B. It is real armour and a permanent target, and it cannot be taken off again. Staying a stranger is the other half of the job.

        THE STAR

        The Sheriff wears it from the first bell — the one role in this mode that is not a secret, and the one man everybody can find. He takes the first go of the round and he has more hits in him than anybody else, and he needs both: the gang knows exactly who to shoot and the law has no idea who to shoot back at. Everybody else is as hidden as they are anywhere else.
